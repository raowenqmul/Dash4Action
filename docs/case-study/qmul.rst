QMUL case study
=====
.. _abstract:

Abstract
------------
Queen Mary University of London recently upgraded one of its datacentres, increasing the power capacity to 390kW from 150kW. A water-base cooling system has been implemented, utilising a hot/cold-aisle design, allowing a heat pump system to efficiently extract the exhaust heat from computing to provide hot water for an on-site district heating network, reusing 60% of heat generated by the datacentre in summer months. This provides an excellent real-world case study for sustainable heat recovery in high performance computing environments,  demonstrating how waste heat from scientific computing can become a valuable energy resource.

Introduction
------------
Queen Mary University of London’s Research Computing Datacentre was initially built in around 2004, commissioned with limited budget and no in-depth prior knowledge. This was a 200kW facility comprising 22 racks of shallow depths. Over the years, all old units broke. With no more spare parts, only 150KW of replacement, temporary chillers were functional. It was clear that the 15 year old facility needed refurbishment. Because of the constraints at QMUL, the only expansion option is to reuse the same machine room.

Method
------------
QMUL’s district heating system supplies around half of the campus using gas-fired boilers. Waste heat from the data centre could be recovered and reused for heating, reducing gas consumption, although heat pumps would require around 30% additional electricity. Following a comparison of costed refurbishment options (with and without heat recovery) and given the constraints, we adopted hot aisle containment with in-row cooling units to improve efficiency.

Result
------------
The refurbishment was completed successfully in autumn 2024, creating 39 racks that are 1200mm deep. The room’s power capacity has been increased to 390kW. An `illustration <srif_>`_ of the cooling is shown below.

Water-based cooling is implemented, using hot aisle containment. Temperature delta is optimised for energy efficiency:  26°C in the cold aisle, 45°C in the hot aisle, 17°C chilled-water input and 23°C chilled-water output. The fluorescent lighting has been replaced with LED lighting.

Resilient N+1 setup has been implemented for power supplies, in-row coolers, heat pumps with alternative heat-exhaust options via dry air coolers or the district-heating heat exchanger.

During normal operation, PUE measured in spring and summer ranges between 1.3-1.8, variation caused by the cooling-plant operating mode (single vs dual heat-pump operation). As shown below, for summer months, about 60% of heat generated by the datacentre flows to the district heating.

Lessons Learned
------------
Having clear, agreed and achievable objectives is essential as the projects involve the senior management, estates, university central IT, and data room IT.

Experienced and dedicated project management (Paddy Hendry from PCH Engineers) familiar with estates and building infrastructure such as cooling, electrical and civil works rather than only IT proved vital.

A concrete financial case and quantified arguments with an expected payback period are central to securing the full project scope, justifying the heat recovery refurbishment option.

Sustainability Impacts
------------
The measured operational data demonstrates the sustainability benefits of integrating heat recovery into a high-density HPC data centre.

The heat is transferred from the datacentre to the district heating system, reducing the reliance on gas-fired boilers and the need to eject heat into the atmosphere. Running the data-centre at two-thirds capacity for 15 years represents a saving of over 8,000 metric tonnes of CO₂.

The water-based cooling system replaced refrigerant gas based cooling solution which can have harmful environmental impacts.

Conclusions
------------
The refurbishment of the datacentre at QMUL demonstrates that heat recovery can be successfully integrated into modern HPC infrastructure, supporting increased compute capacity while maintaining sustainable operation. Over summer months, 60% of the generated heat is transferred to the district heating system.

Long-term measurements of power, energy, temperatures, and cooling performance under production workloads provide valuable evidence for evaluating the effectiveness of heat recovery technologies and informing the design of future sustainable data centres.
